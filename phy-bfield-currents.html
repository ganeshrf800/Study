Moving Charges and Magnetism

Magnetic field of currents: Biot-Savart, Ampere's law, parallel wires

Moving charges produce magnetic fields. The Biot-Savart law adds up the contribution of every small piece of current, and Ampere's law gives a shortcut when the situation is symmetric. These two give the fields of a straight wire, a circular loop and a solenoid, and explain why parallel currents attract.

  • Biot-Savart: each current element I dl gives dB = (μ₀/4π) I dl sinθ / r². The direction is given by the right-hand rule.
  • Long straight wire: B = μ₀I/(2πr), falling as 1/r. Field lines are circles around the wire.
  • Ampere's law: ∮B·dl = μ₀Ienclosed. For an ideal solenoid it gives a uniform B = μ₀nI inside and almost zero outside.
  • Parallel currents in the same direction attract; opposite currents repel. This defines the ampere.

In the real world

A solenoid wound with 500 turns over 25 cm has n = 500/0.25 = 2000 turns per metre. With I = 2 A the field inside is B = μ₀nI = 4π×10⁻⁷ × 2000 × 2 ≈ 5.0×10⁻³ T = 5 mT, about 100 times Earth's field of roughly 4×10⁻⁵ T. Put a soft iron core inside and the field rises by a factor of hundreds; this is how scrapyard electromagnet cranes lift steel.

Two parallel wires 1 m apart, each carrying 1 A in vacuum, attract with F/L = 2×10⁻⁷ N per metre. That value is the definition of the ampere in older SI. A 100 A overhead cable seen from 10 m below produces only B = 2×10⁻⁷ × 100/10 = 2×10⁻⁶ T = 2 µT, much smaller than Earth's field.

Two long parallel wires carry currents in the same direction. What happens?

The field of wire 1 at wire 2 is perpendicular to wire 2, and F = I₂L × B points toward wire 1. The same argument holds the other way, so the force is attractive. The wires are neutral, but the magnetic force acts on the moving charges.
