Application of Derivatives

Rates, tangents, monotonicity and optimisation

The derivative tells you how fast a quantity changes and which way. Where it is positive the function rises, where negative it falls, and where it passes through zero the function may peak or dip. That is how engineers find the cheapest tin or the biggest box.

  • dy/dx is the rate of change of y with respect to x. With related rates, differentiate with respect to time t using the chain rule.
  • The tangent at (x₁, y₁) has slope f′(x₁); the normal is perpendicular, with slope −1/f′(x₁).
  • f′ > 0 on an interval means strictly increasing; f′ < 0 means strictly decreasing.
  • Critical points satisfy f′(c) = 0. The second-derivative test: f″(c) < 0 is a local maximum, f″(c) > 0 a local minimum.
  • For the absolute maximum or minimum on [a, b], compare values at critical points and at the end points.

In the real world

A packaging unit wants a closed cylindrical tin holding 1 litre (1000 cm³) using the least metal. With V = πr²h = 1000, h = 1000/(πr²) and the surface area is S = 2πr² + 2000/r. Setting S′ = 4πr − 2000/r² = 0 gives r³ = 500/π, so r ≈ 5.42 cm and h = 2r ≈ 10.84 cm. Then S ≈ 553.6 cm². S″ = 4π + 4000/r³ > 0, so this is a minimum. Compare a thinner tin with r = 4 cm: h ≈ 19.9 cm and S = 2π(16) + 500 ≈ 600.5 cm², about 8.5% more metal. Real tins are often taller than h = 2r for shelf-fit and handling, which costs a little extra material.

For the 200 m fence along a river, A(x) = x(200 − 2x). What maximises the area?

A′(x) = 200 − 4x = 0 gives x = 50 and A″ = −4 < 0 (maximum). Length = 200 − 100 = 100 m and A = 5000 m². The other options give smaller areas: x = 100 gives 0, x = 40 gives 40 × 120 = 4800 and x = 25 gives 25 × 150 = 3750.
