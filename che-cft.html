Crystal field theory: splitting, spin state and colour

Crystal field theory treats ligands as negative point charges that repel the metal's d electrons. In an octahedral complex the five d orbitals split into a lower t2g set (3 orbitals) and an upper eg set (2 orbitals) separated by Δo. How electrons occupy these levels decides the magnetism and the colour.

  • The eg orbitals (dz², dx²−y²) point at the ligands and are raised; the t2g orbitals (dxy, dyz, dzx) point between them and are lowered. Relative to the average, t2g sits at −0.4 Δo and eg at +0.6 Δo.
  • For d4 to d7 there is a choice. If Δo < P (pairing energy), electrons occupy eg before pairing: weak-field, high-spin. If Δo > P they pair in t2g first: strong-field, low-spin. d1–d3 and d8–d10 have only one arrangement.
  • Spectrochemical series (increasing Δ): I− < Br− < SCN− < Cl− < S2− < F− < OH− < C2O42− < H2O < NCS− < edta4− < NH3 < en < CN− < CO.
  • Tetrahedral splitting is smaller: Δt = (4/9)Δo, always less than P in practice, so tetrahedral complexes are high-spin.
  • Colour: the complex absorbs light whose energy equals Δ (a d–d transition) and appears in the complementary colour. d0 and d10 have no d–d transition and are colourless.

In the real world

Worked example, [Ti(H2O)6]3+. Ti3+ is d1, with its electron in t2g. Absorption peaks at about 20 300 cm⁻¹, so λ = 107 / 20 300 ≈ 493 nm (blue-green). The ion transmits the rest and looks violet. Δo = 20 300 × 0.01196 ≈ 243 kJ mol⁻¹. CFSE = −0.4 Δo ≈ −97 kJ mol⁻¹.

Ruby and emerald. Both get their colour from Cr3+ (d3) in an octahedral oxygen environment. In ruby (Al2O3 host) the Cr–O distance is shorter, so Δo is larger; it absorbs yellow-green and blue-violet and the stone looks red. In emerald (beryl host, Be3Al2Si6O18) Δo is a little smaller; it absorbs red and violet and looks green.

Oxygen transport. Deoxyhaemoglobin has high-spin Fe2+ (d6, four unpaired electrons); when O2 binds, the field gets stronger and the iron becomes low-spin (no unpaired electrons) and slightly smaller. That small change triggers the shape change that lets haemoglobin bind oxygen cooperatively.

How many unpaired electrons are in [Fe(CN)6]4−?

Fe is +2 here, d⁶. CN⁻ is a strong-field ligand (Δo > P), so all six electrons pair up in t₂g: t₂g⁶ e_g⁰, diamagnetic. With a weak-field ligand like H₂O, [Fe(H₂O)₆]²⁺ is t₂g⁴e_g² with 4 unpaired electrons.
