Unit cells, packing efficiency and density

A crystalline solid is built by repeating a small box, the unit cell, in three dimensions. Counting how many atoms belong to one cell, and how much of the cell they fill, lets you predict density from the edge length. The three cubic cells are simple (primitive), body-centred (bcc) and face-centred (fcc).

  • An atom at a corner is shared by 8 cells (contributes 1/8), on a face by 2 (1/2), on an edge by 4 (1/4), inside the cell fully (1).
  • Atoms per cell Z: simple cubic 1, bcc 2, fcc 4.
  • Atoms touch along the edge (simple cubic), the body diagonal (bcc) or the face diagonal (fcc), which fixes r in terms of a.
  • Packing efficiency: simple cubic 52.4%, bcc 68%, fcc (and hcp) 74%. Coordination numbers: 6, 8, 12.

In the real world

Aluminium in cookware and coaches. Aluminium is fcc with a = 405 pm and M = 26.98 g/mol. Z = 4, a = 4.05×10⁻⁸ cm, so a³ = 6.64×10⁻²³ cm³. ρ = 4 × 26.98 / (6.64×10⁻²³ × 6.022×10²³) = 107.9 / 40.0 = 2.70 g/cm³, matching the measured value. This low density is why aluminium is used for lightweight railway coaches and pressure cookers.

Why steel can be hardened. Iron is bcc (68% packed) at room temperature but turns fcc (74% packed) above about 912 °C. Carbon atoms dissolve in the larger gaps of the fcc form, and quenching traps them, which is the basis of hardening.

How many atoms belong to one face-centred cubic unit cell?

8 corners × 1/8 = 1, plus 6 face centres × 1/2 = 3, giving 4 atoms. (14 is the number of lattice points you can see, not the number owned by the cell.)
