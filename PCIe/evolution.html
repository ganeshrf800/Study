    02 / Signal

    NRZ and PAM4: how bits become voltage

    In plain words

    Gen1 to Gen5 use NRZ: the wire is either high or low, one bit per tick, like a light switch. Gen6 onward use PAM4: four voltage levels, so each tick carries two bits, like a dimmer with four positions. You get double the data without doubling the tick rate, but the levels sit three times closer together, so noise causes more mistakes.

    Example

    Gen5 sends 32 billion ticks a second at one bit each. Gen6 keeps 32 billion ticks but sends two bits per tick, giving 64 Gbit/s. The price: raw errors rise from about 1 in a trillion bits to about 1 in a million, which is why Gen6 adds error correction (section 06).

    03 / Encoding

    8b/10b, 128b/130b and FLITs

    In plain words

    Raw data can contain long runs of zeros, and the receiver needs regular changes to keep time. Encoding adds extra bits to guarantee changes and to mark where things start. 8b/10b spends 2 extra bits on every 8, a 20% tax. 128b/130b spends 2 bits on every 128 and scrambles the rest, a 1.5% tax. Gen6 drops the per-block bits and packs everything into fixed 256-byte FLITs.

    Example

    Gen2 runs at 5 GT/s but delivers only 4 Gbit/s of data. If Gen3 had kept 8b/10b it would have needed 10 GT/s to double Gen2. By changing the encoding, 8 GT/s was enough: 8 × 128/130 = 7.88 Gbit/s. A slower signal was far easier to send over cheap circuit boards.

    How much of the line is your data?

    Dark part is data; the light remainder is encoding overhead. FLIT overhead is the CRC and error-correction bytes in each 256-byte unit.

    04 / Equalization

    Fixing a signal smeared by the board

    In plain words

    A circuit-board trace acts like a muffler: fast changes get weaker than slow ones. After a long run of 1s, a single 0 barely dips before the signal goes back up. The cure is to exaggerate the changes at the transmitter (shout the first bit after a change, then ease off) and to boost and clean up at the receiver. Gen1 and Gen2 use one fixed setting. From Gen3, the two ends negotiate the best setting every time the link trains.

    Example

    Move the loss slider right until the grey received signal no longer crosses the centre line on lone bits. Then add de-emphasis: the coloured trace crosses cleanly again. That is what the link does by itself during training, trying presets P0 to P10 until the receiver is happy.

    05 / Packets

    Anatomy of a packet, then and now

    In plain words

    Every read or write travels as a TLP (Transaction Layer Packet): an envelope with an address (header), contents (data) and a tamper seal (CRC). The lower layers add a serial number and start/end markers. Up to Gen5, each packet is framed individually. In Gen6, packets are poured into fixed-size 256-byte containers (FLITs), like parcels loaded into identical crates.

    Example

    When an NVMe SSD returns a 4 KB block, it sends a "completion with data" TLP. With a 512-byte Max Payload Size, that is 8 TLPs, each with its own header and checks. Raising the payload size means fewer headers and less overhead; try the slider below.

    06 / Reliability

    Credits, ACK/NAK and error correction

    In plain words

    Two rules keep data safe. Flow control: the sender may only send when the receiver has announced free space (credits), like only sending as many parcels as there are empty shelves. ACK/NAK: the sender keeps a copy of each packet until the receiver confirms it arrived intact; if the check fails, the receiver says NAK and the sender replays it. Gen6 adds FEC, which repairs most errors on the spot without a resend.

    Example

    A marginal riser cable corrupts one packet in a million. You never see wrong data: the link replays it within microseconds. What you do see is a rising "correctable error" count in the system log and slightly worse latency. That count is the early warning.

    08 / In practice

    Real devices and what they use

    Slot sizes you can recognise by eye

    Connector lengths are drawn to scale. A shorter card fits a longer slot. The slot's physical length does not guarantee its electrical width: an x16-length slot is sometimes wired for only x4 or x8, so check the board manual.

    Rule of thumb

    The link runs at the lower generation and the narrower width of the two ends. A Gen5 x4 SSD in a Gen3 x4 slot runs Gen3 x4. A Gen4 x16 GPU in a slot wired x8 runs Gen4 x8.

    Worked example

    You buy a Gen5 SSD rated 14 GB/s for a 2021 laptop whose slot is Gen4. Ceiling: 16 GT/s × 128/130 ÷ 8 × 4 lanes = 7.9 GB/s. The drive works, but you get about 7 GB/s, half of what you paid for.
