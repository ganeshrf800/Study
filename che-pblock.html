The p-Block Elements

Group 15, 16, 17 and 18: trends and anomalies

In p-block elements the last electron enters a p orbital. Groups 15 to 18 (the pnictogens, chalcogens, halogens and noble gases) show clear trends down each group, but the first member of each group (N, O, F) is anomalous because it is small, highly electronegative and has no d orbitals.

  • Valence configurations: group 15 ns²np³, group 16 ns²np⁴, group 17 ns²np⁵, group 18 ns²np⁶ (He is 1s²).
  • Inert pair effect: down the group the lower oxidation state (two units below the group maximum) becomes more stable. In group 15, +3 is more stable than +5 for Bi; in group 16, +4 is more stable than +6 for Te and Po.
  • Hydrides of groups 15 and 16: bond angle falls and stability falls down the group (NH3 107°, PH3 93.5°; H2O 104.5°, H2S 92°). Acidity rises: HF < HCl < HBr < HI, and H2O < H2S < H2Se < H2Te.
  • Halogens: F2 is the strongest oxidising agent; Cl2 displaces Br− and I−. Fluorine shows only −1; others show +1, +3, +5, +7 in compounds with more electronegative atoms (oxoacids, interhalogens).
  • Noble gases have closed shells, very high ionisation enthalpy and positive electron gain enthalpy. Neil Bartlett (1962) made Xe+[PtF6]−; XeF2, XeF4, XeF6 are now well known.

In the real world

Worked example: what does −349 kJ/mol mean for one atom? Chlorine releases 349 kJ when one mole of gaseous atoms each gains an electron. Per atom: 349 000 J ÷ 6.022×1023 = 5.80×10−19 J, and 5.80×10−19 ÷ 1.602×10−19 J/eV ≈ 3.6 eV. Compare I− formation at −296 kJ/mol ≈ 3.1 eV, the reason Cl2 oxidises iodide.

Group 17 in daily Indian life. Fluoride in toothpaste and in water-fluoridation limits (above 1.5 mg/L causes fluorosis in parts of Rajasthan and Andhra Pradesh); chlorine to disinfect municipal water; iodised salt (KIO3) against goitre; bromides in photography and medicines.

Group 18. Argon fills incandescent bulbs, helium is mixed with oxygen for deep-sea divers and used to cool MRI magnets, and neon lights glow red-orange.

Which statement about electron gain enthalpy of halogens is correct?

Cl (−349 kJ/mol) beats F (−333 kJ/mol). F's 2p subshell is so compact that the new electron is repelled strongly. Electronegativity and electron gain enthalpy are related but not the same property.
