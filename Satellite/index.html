PhoneSat-LTE: small imaging satellite to mobile phone — design and analysis

16 November 2026

1. Mission and requirements

PhoneSat-LTE is a 12U CubeSat that photographs a commanded spot on Earth and transmits the picture straight to an unmodified LTE smartphone, acting as a cell tower in orbit for the few minutes it is overhead. A ground-station path delivers the same picture over the ordinary mobile network when the phone is not under the beam.

"Normal mobile" is read here as: stock phone hardware and firmware, no external antenna, no special app. The phone still needs a SIM or eSIM profile the satellite's network accepts, as with any LTE network.

2. Architecture trade

The selected design is option A with option B built in: the satellite is itself a small LTE base station, and its S-band ground link doubles as the fallback delivery path. Received power at the phone depends only on satellite transmit power times antenna area, not on the band, so the design is driven by how much flat antenna a small satellite can unfold.

PhoneSat-LTE sits at the Lynk end of this range: a 0.53 m² array, one 1.4 MHz LTE carrier, one phone-sized spot. That is enough for a 0.5 MB picture in about 8 s.

Phones built to 3GPP Release 17 non-terrestrial-network rules compensate Doppler and delay themselves, but those rules cover NB-IoT, eMTC and new satellite bands, not legacy LTE handsets. For a normal phone the satellite must do all the compensation, which section 5 sizes.

3. System at a glance

The highlighted path is the direct one: camera, payload computer, LTE array, phone. The lower path through S-band, the ground station and the mobile network carries commands up and is the fallback for pictures. The power system feeds every unit.

5. Communications design

Both LTE links close at 40° elevation with 3–5 dB margin, and the first-contact (random access) signal is the weakest at 2.4 dB, so attach is scheduled above 50° where it has 3.7 dB. The S-band ground link has 11 dB margin at 2 Mbit/s.

Resulting rates: about 490 kbit/s to the phone at 40° and about 950 kbit/s near zenith, where the downlink signal-to-noise is 7.9 dB. The phone-sized image takes about 8 s and the full-resolution JPEG about 57 s at the lower rate. Phone signal level (RSRP) is −117 to −121 dBm: a weak but usable one-bar cell.

The uplink only carries attach signalling and acknowledgements, at tens of kbit/s. The required values are typical receiver thresholds, to be replaced by measured ones after the emulator campaign in section 10.

5.4 Network and delivery to the user

  1. The phone holds a PhoneSat eSIM profile (demonstration) or a partner operator's SIM. Hardware and firmware are untouched.

  2. Subscriber authentication data for expected phones is loaded to the satellite over S-band before the pass, so the on-board core can authenticate without a live ground link.

  3. When the beam arrives the phone finds the cell, attaches, and gets an IP address.

  4. The satellite sends a text message over LTE signalling: a link to the picture.

  5. The user taps the link; the phone's browser downloads the JPEG from the satellite's on-board web server.

Text-plus-link is used because every LTE phone supports SMS over signalling without operator-specific settings. Picture messaging (MMS) is a later option once an operator's settings are in place.

8. Design drawings

These are concept-level layout drawings with the controlling dimensions; the two views of the first drawing are at different scales. Detailed mechanical drawings follow in the detailed-design phase.

Each wing's inner panel hinges on the nadir edge of the bus and the outer panel folds back over it, so the antenna patches face each other when stowed and the solar cells of the outer panels face outward. That gives power before deployment. All 48 patches then face the ground and all cells face the sky.

The camera looks out through the centre of the nadir face, on the same axis as the antenna beam. Heavy items sit mid-length to keep the centre of gravity inside the dispenser limits.

9. Software and operations

The satellite spends almost all its time sun-pointed in standby and leaves it only for scheduled, time-tagged activities uploaded from the ground. Any fault returns it to safe mode.

Image pipeline on the payload computer:

  1. Capture a burst of 3 frames at the target; keep the sharpest.

  2. Correct dark level and flat field; demosaic; white-balance.

  3. Tag with time, position and pointing; compute corner coordinates.

  4. Produce the 2048 × 1500 JPEG for the phone and a losslessly compressed raw for the ground.

  5. Queue both; delete only after ground acknowledgement.

Delivery sequence for one request:

  1. A user requests a picture of a location through a web page or text message on the ordinary mobile network, giving the phone's delivery location.

  2. Mission planning schedules the capture and the next pass above 50° over the phone, and uploads commands and the phone's authentication data.

  3. The satellite captures and processes the image.

  4. On the delivery pass it slews to track the phone's location, switches on the LTE carrier inside the licensed area, and waits for attach.

  5. It sends the text with the link, serves the download, and logs the result.

  6. If the phone never attaches, the image goes down on the next ground pass and is sent by the server over the terrestrial network.

Flight software runs on a real-time operating system with a command scheduler, telemetry store, fault monitor and bootloader holding two software images. The LTE stack is an adapted open-source eNodeB and core, with added Doppler and timing pre-compensation and a modified random-access detector.

10. Verification: tests and methodology

Every requirement is closed by test, analysis, inspection or demonstration, on two hardware models: an engineering model (bench "FlatSat" plus one full antenna wing) and a single protoflight model that is tested at qualification levels for acceptance durations and then flown. Environmental levels below are generic starting values; the launch provider's levels replace them once the launch is booked.

10.1 Method

  1. Each requirement in section 1 and each specification in sections 4–6 gets a row in a verification matrix: method, model, pass criterion, report.

  2. Tests run bottom-up: component, subsystem, payload, then spacecraft.

  3. A short functional test runs before and after every environmental exposure, and results are compared.

  4. Any failure is logged as a non-conformance and the test is repeated after the fix.

  5. Analyses (links, thermal, structural, orbit lifetime) are correlated against test data before launch.

11. Licensing and regulation

Spectrum access is the hardest non-technical item: a satellite may transmit on a mobile band only with the cooperation of the operator licensed for it. The table assumes a US-licensed mission; other countries have equivalent regimes.

Disposal by drag needs care because the nadir-pointed satellite flies its panels edge-on, with only about 0.08 m² facing the airflow. At end of mission it is turned to fly the 0.6 m² array face-on, and a dead, tumbling satellite presents about 0.3 m² on average. Both give a ballistic coefficient of roughly 17–30 kg/m² and an expected decay from 500 km in about 1–4 years depending on solar activity. This is an estimate and must be confirmed with a standard lifetime tool (NASA DAS or ESA DRAMA) before the licence filing.
