The communication model

Every communication system, from two tin cans on a string to a 5G network, has the same five parts. A source produces information. A transmitter turns it into a signal suited to the medium. The channel carries the signal and also weakens it, distorts it and adds noise. A receiver undoes as much of that damage as it can, and the destination gets the recovered information.

Signals, frequency and bandwidth

  • Analog and digital. An analog signal varies continuously (a microphone voltage). A digital signal is a stream of bits. Modern mobile systems digitise everything, because bits can be compressed, protected with error-correcting codes and regenerated without accumulating noise.
  • Frequency (f) is how many cycles a wave completes per second, in hertz. Wavelength (λ) is the distance one cycle covers: λ = c / f, with c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s. At 900 MHz the wavelength is 33 cm; at 3.5 GHz it is 8.6 cm; at 28 GHz it is 1.07 cm. Antenna size scales with wavelength.
  • Bandwidth (B) is the range of frequencies a signal occupies. More bandwidth carries more data. A GSM channel is 200 kHz wide, an LTE carrier up to 20 MHz, a 5G mid-band carrier up to 100 MHz.
  • Baseband and passband. Information starts as a baseband signal centred on 0 Hz. To radiate it from a small antenna and to share the air with other users, it is shifted up onto a high-frequency carrier. That shift is modulation plus up-conversion.

Noise, SNR and the Shannon limit

Every receiver sees thermal noise. At room temperature its density is −174 dBm/Hz, so the noise in a bandwidth B is −174 + 10·log₁₀(B) dBm. The receiver's own electronics add a little more, described by its noise figure (NF). The ratio of signal power to noise power is the SNR.

Claude Shannon showed in 1948 that a channel of bandwidth B and a given SNR has a hard upper limit on error-free data rate:

C = B · log₂(1 + SNR)    bits per second (SNR as a plain ratio, not dB)

There are only two ways to go faster: more bandwidth or more SNR. (Multiple antennas, covered later, create several parallel channels and multiply the limit.)

The wireless channel

In a wireless link the channel is free space plus everything in it. Compared with a cable the channel is far lossier, changes from millisecond to millisecond, and is shared with every other transmitter nearby. Most of the design effort in mobile systems goes into coping with those three facts.

Propagation

  • Free-space path loss. Power spreads over a growing sphere, so received power falls with the square of distance. In convenient units: FSPL (dB) = 32.44 + 20·log₁₀(d in km) + 20·log₁₀(f in MHz) Doubling distance or frequency costs 6 dB.
  • Shadowing. Buildings, hills and walls block the signal, adding 10–30 dB of slowly varying loss.
  • Multipath fading. Reflections arrive with different delays and add or cancel. The signal can dip 20–30 dB within half a wavelength of movement. Delayed copies also smear one symbol into the next (inter-symbol interference).
  • Doppler shift. Motion shifts the carrier by f·v/c. At 2 GHz and 120 km/h that is about 220 Hz.

Engineering link budget: maximum allowable path loss

Network dimensioning works backwards from the receiver: given the SINR the chosen MCS needs at the cell edge, how much path loss can the link tolerate? The maximum allowable path loss (MAPL) of uplink and downlink is computed separately; the smaller one limits the cell radius. In mid-band TDD it is almost always the uplink, because the phone's 23 dBm faces the same path as the base station's 200 W.

MAPL = EIRP + G_rx − L_rx − S_rx − M_interference − M_shadow − L_penetration − L_body    S_rx = −174 + 10 log B + NF + SINR_req

The shadow-fading margin converts a median path loss into an edge-coverage probability: M_shadow = σ_SF · Φ⁻¹(p_edge), i.e. 1.28 σ for 90 % edge reliability (about 97 % area reliability for typical slopes).

Constellation and EVM

A constellation plots every symbol the transmitter can send as a point on the I/Q plane. The receiver decides which point was sent by picking the nearest one, so the spacing between points, compared with the size of the noise cloud, sets the error rate. Packing in more points carries more bits per symbol but needs a cleaner signal.

Each dot is one received symbol. Noise spreads the dots into clouds around the ideal points. When the clouds overlap, the receiver mistakes one symbol for its neighbour. How each scheme looks in frequency

A signal can be described in time (a waveform) or in frequency (a spectrum). The two are tied together: the faster the symbols change, the wider the spectrum. Three things decide what the spectrum looks like:

  • Symbol rate sets the width. The main lobe is roughly as wide as the symbol rate, which is why 16QAM at the same bit rate as QPSK needs half the bandwidth.
  • Pulse shape sets the skirts. Abrupt symbol transitions splash energy far into neighbouring channels; a pulse-shaping filter confines it.
  • Modulation order (QPSK, 16QAM, 64QAM) does not change the shape. It changes how many bits each symbol carries and how much the envelope varies.

OFDM: the waveform of 4G and 5G

Instead of one fast stream of symbols on one carrier, orthogonal frequency-division multiplexing sends many slow streams in parallel on closely spaced subcarriers, each carrying its own QAM symbol. An inverse FFT builds the waveform; an FFT in the receiver separates the subcarriers again.

  • Slow symbols are long compared with multipath delays. A short guard copy of each symbol's tail, the cyclic prefix, absorbs the echoes.
  • Subcarriers can be given to different users, which is OFDMA. LTE groups 12 subcarriers of 15 kHz into a 180 kHz resource block.
  • The drawback is a high peak-to-average power ratio (PAPR), 8–12 dB, which forces the power amplifier to back off from its efficient region. The LTE uplink therefore uses SC-FDMA (also called DFT-s-OFDM), which has 2–3 dB lower PAPR. The 5G uplink can use either.

With no offset, every other subcarrier passes through zero exactly where the wanted one peaks: they overlap without interfering. That is what orthogonal means, and it works because the spacing equals 1 ÷ symbol length. Slide the offset up and the zeros no longer line up, so neighbours leak in. This is why a phone must hold its carrier within ± 0.1 ppm, and why 5G uses wider spacing at higher frequencies, where Doppler and oscillator drift are larger in hertz.

Coding and MIMO

  • Channel coding adds redundant bits so the receiver can correct errors. 2G used convolutional codes, 3G and 4G use turbo codes, 5G uses LDPC for data and polar codes for control. The code rate is the fraction of useful bits.
  • HARQ retransmits only when decoding fails and combines the retry with the first attempt.
  • MIMO sends several data streams from several antennas on the same frequency. It has its own tab: MIMO and CA.

MIMO: using more than one antenna

Bandwidth and SNR are the two levers in Shannon's formula. Multiple antennas add a third: space. MIMO stands for multiple-input multiple-output, where "input" and "output" refer to the radio channel. Terms you will meet

  • Layer: one independent data stream. Rank: how many layers the channel can support right now.
  • Precoding: the weights that map layers onto antennas. The phone recommends a precoder from a standard codebook.
  • CSI feedback: the phone reports rank (RI), preferred precoder (PMI) and channel quality (CQI); the base station then picks layers, precoder and modulation for the next slots.
  • Reference signals: known symbols used to measure the channel. CSI-RS for downlink measurement, DMRS for demodulating each layer, SRS sent by the phone so the base station can measure the uplink.
  • SU-MIMO and MU-MIMO: all layers to one phone (single user), or layers shared between phones in different directions (multi user).

Spatial multiplexing and layers

Every transmit antenna reaches every receive antenna over a slightly different path. Those paths form the channel matrix H. If the paths differ enough, the receiver can solve for the individual streams, like solving simultaneous equations. In an ideal, perfectly separable channel with the transmit power shared equally between layers:

C = L · B · log₂(1 + SNR · N_r / L)     L = layers ≤ min(N_t, N_r)

Each added layer multiplies the rate but divides the power, so layers pay off only when the SNR is good. At the cell edge one well-combined layer beats four starved ones. This is why the network adapts the rank continuously.

What limits the number of layers

  • Scattering. Layers need paths that look different. Reflections help; a clear line of sight does not.
  • Antenna correlation. Antennas closer than about half a wavelength see nearly the same signal. In a phone at 700 MHz (43 cm wavelength) there is only room for two useful antennas; at 3.5 GHz four fit comfortably.
  • SNR. As the calculator shows, splitting power only pays when there is power to spare.

Precoding and CSI in NR

  • CSI framework. CSI-RS resources (up to 32 ports), CSI-IM for interference measurement, reporting of CRI, RI, PMI, LI and CQI, periodic on PUCCH or aperiodic on PUSCH.
  • Type I single-panel codebook. 2D DFT beams over (N1, N2) ports per polarisation with oversampling (O1, O2); i1 selects a wideband beam group, i2 the beam and cross-polarisation co-phasing per subband. Up to 8 layers; coarse spatial resolution, suited to SU-MIMO.
  • Type II codebook. Linear combination of L = 2–4 orthogonal beams with per-subband amplitude and phase. Much finer CSI for MU-MIMO at a large feedback cost; Rel-16 eType II compresses it in the frequency domain (and supports rank 4).
  • Reciprocity (TDD). The gNB estimates the downlink channel from SRS; with 1T4R or 2T4R antenna switching the full 4-antenna channel is sounded. Eigen-beamforming or regularised ZF across users follows.
  • Hybrid beamforming (FR2). Analog beams per panel from a codebook chosen by beam sweeping (P1/P2/P3 procedures), digital precoding across a few RF chains.

Antenna correlation

From the S-parameters of a two-port antenna system, the envelope correlation coefficient is

ECC = |S₁₁*S₁₂ + S₂₁*S₂₂|² / [(1 − |S₁₁|² − |S₂₁|²)(1 − |S₂₂|² − |S₁₂|²)]

What MIMO costs in hardware

  • Four receive chains per band group: four antennas, four filters, four LNAs and four transceiver receive paths. The primary and diversity paths are joined by two more "MIMO" paths.
  • Antenna design. The antennas must be efficient and also uncorrelated with each other, measured by the envelope correlation coefficient. Isolation between them matters too.
  • Uplink MIMO needs two power amplifiers transmitting together. A cheaper alternative, uplink transmit switching, moves one transmitter between carriers.
  • SRS antenna switching (written 1T4R or 2T4R): the phone transmits its sounding signal from each receive antenna in turn so that a TDD base station can beamform to all four.
  • Baseband load. Channel estimation and separating four layers across 100 MHz is among the heaviest jobs in the modem.

How MIMO is tested

  • Receiver sensitivity is measured per antenna port and with all ports combined.
  • Demodulation tests (TS 38.101-4) check throughput for 2 and 4 layers through a fading channel emulator with defined correlation between antennas, and check that the reported rank and precoder are sensible.
  • Radiated MIMO tests place the whole phone in a chamber that recreates a multipath environment, so the real antennas are included.

Carrier aggregation

An operator rarely owns one wide block of spectrum. It owns pieces: 10 MHz at 700 MHz, 20 MHz at 1800 MHz, 100 MHz at 3.5 GHz. Carrier aggregation (CA) lets one phone use several of these carriers at the same time, as if they were one wide channel. Each carrier is a component carrier (CC).

  • The primary cell (PCell) carries the connection: signalling, mobility and the uplink control channel.
  • Secondary cells (SCells) are added when there is data to send and switched off within milliseconds when there is not, to save battery.
  • One scheduler in the base station spreads the data across all carriers slot by slot, so the benefit is both a higher peak rate and better use of each band: low band for reach, mid band for capacity.

How the specifications handle it

  • Carrier-aggregation requirements sit in the "A" sub-clauses of TS 38.101-1 (for example 6.2A for output power and 7.3A for reference sensitivity). EN-DC and FR1 + FR2 combinations are in TS 38.101-3.

NR frame structure and physical channels

Time–frequency structure

  • Frame 10 ms → 10 subframes of 1 ms → 2μ slots per subframe → 14 OFDM symbols per slot (normal CP; 12 with extended CP at 60 kHz only).
  • Basic time unit Tc = 1/(480 kHz · 4096) ≈ 0.509 ns. Normal CP is 144·κ·2−μ·Tc (κ = 64): 4.69 µs at 15 kHz, with a longer CP on the first symbol of every 0.5 ms.
  • Resource element = one subcarrier × one symbol; PRB = 12 subcarriers. A bandwidth part (BWP) is a contiguous set of PRBs with its own numerology; up to four per direction are configured and one is active, which lets a UE run a narrow BWP to save power.

SS/PBCH block and beam sweeping

An SSB spans 4 symbols × 240 subcarriers (20 PRB). Up to Lmax = 4 (≤ 3 GHz), 8 (3–6 GHz) or 64 (FR2) SSBs, each on a different beam, are sent within a 5 ms half-frame; the burst repeats every 5–160 ms (a UE assumes 20 ms during initial search). SSBs sit on a sparse synchronisation raster (GSCN), not on the channel raster, which shortens cell search across wide bands.

Initial access, RRC states and mobility

From power-on to data

  1. Cell search. Scan GSCN raster points; detect PSS (symbol timing, NID(2)), then SSS (NID(1), hence PCI).
  2. System information. Decode PBCH/MIB, locate CORESET#0, decode SIB1 (cell access, RACH configuration, TDD pattern, other SIB scheduling).
  3. Random access, 4-step. Msg1: preamble on a RACH occasion tied to the best SSB beam. Msg2: random-access response with timing advance, UL grant and temporary C-RNTI. Msg3: RRCSetupRequest. Msg4: RRCSetup and contention resolution. Rel-16 adds a 2-step procedure (MsgA = preamble + PUSCH, MsgB).
  4. RRC and NAS. RRCSetupComplete carries the NAS registration request to the AMF; authentication and security mode follow, then a PDU session is established through the SMF and UPF.
  5. Connected operation. RRC reconfiguration adds SCells, BWPs, CSI and SRS configurations, MIMO layers and DRX.

  • SS-RSRP: linear average power per RE of the SSS (reported −156 to −31 dBm). SS-RSRQ = N·RSRP / RSSI over N PRBs. SS-SINR excludes noise-only REs.

Power amplifier: efficiency, linearity and linearisation

Figures of merit

  • PAE = (Pout − Pin)/PDC. Drain (collector) efficiency ignores the drive power.
  • Back-off: average output power below P1dB or Psat. A modulated signal with PAPR x dB needs roughly x − (CFR, DPD allowances) dB of back-off; efficiency of a class-AB stage falls steeply with back-off.
  • Linearity: AM-AM (gain compression) and AM-PM (phase rotation with amplitude) produce IMD3/IMD5 and spectral regrowth; memory effects (bias network, thermal) make distortion depend on signal history and bandwidth.
  • Load sensitivity: antenna VSWR changes with hand and head; PAs are load-pull characterised and must survive high VSWR at all phases. Antenna tuners and coupler-based closed-loop power control keep radiated power stable.

Digital pre-distortion

DPD applies the inverse of the PA's nonlinearity in the digital domain. A widely used form is the memory polynomial

y(n) = Σₖ Σₘ a_km · x(n − m) · |x(n − m)|^(k−1)

with odd orders k ≤ 7 and memory depth m ≤ 3–5. Coefficients are estimated from a feedback (observation) receiver through the output coupler, by indirect learning or direct least squares. Handsets often use look-up-table DPD per power level and band; base stations adapt continuously. Crest-factor reduction (peak windowing or clipping and filtering) lowers PAPR by a few dB before DPD, at a controlled EVM cost.

  • RF front-end. Modules grouped by band range (low, mid, high, ultra-high). A PAMiD integrates PA, duplexers, switches and often the LNA. Separate diversity receive modules serve the secondary antennas.
  • Antennas. Four to eight cellular antennas around the frame, shared between bands through switches, diplexers and tuners.

Features that multiply the number of paths (see the MIMO and CA tab): carrier aggregation (several carriers at once), 4×4 downlink MIMO, EN-DC (LTE and NR transmitting together in non-standalone 5G), and uplink MIMO or transmit switching. Gain budget analysis

A gain budget (also called a cascade or line-up analysis) walks through the chain stage by stage and tracks three things: how big the signal is, how much noise has been added, and how much distortion the chain will produce. It is how a designer decides how much gain, noise figure and linearity each block must have.

The three cascade rules

  • Gain adds in dB: G_total = G₁ + G₂ + G₃ + …
  • Noise figure follows the Friis formula (F and G as plain ratios): F_total = F₁ + (F₂ − 1)/G₁ + (F₃ − 1)/(G₁·G₂) + … The first stages dominate. Loss before the LNA adds dB for dB; noise after a high-gain LNA is divided down. This is why the LNA sits as close to the antenna as possible.
  • Linearity (input third-order intercept, in mW): 1/IIP3_total = 1/IIP3₁ + G₁/IIP3₂ + G₁·G₂/IIP3₃ + … The last stages dominate, because they see the largest signals. More front-end gain helps noise and hurts linearity; the budget is the search for the balance.

From the totals come the headline receiver numbers:

Sensitivity = −174 dBm/Hz + 10·log₁₀(B) + NF_total + SNR_required Spurious-free dynamic range = ⅔ · (IIP3_total − noise floor)

Building an uncertainty budget (GUM)

  1. List every contributor: instrument absolute accuracy, mismatch, path-loss calibration, repeatability, temperature drift, cable flexing.
  2. Convert each to a standard uncertainty: divide a ± limit by √3 for a rectangular distribution, by √2 for U-shaped (mismatch), by the coverage factor for a normal distribution quoted at 95 % (k = 2).
  3. Combine in quadrature: u_c = √(Σ uᵢ²). Report the expanded uncertainty U = k·u_c with k = 2 (≈ 95 % confidence).
  4. Mismatch between source and load reflection coefficients ΓS, ΓL has limits 20 log(1 ± |Γ_S||Γ_L|) dB.

Statistical testing of throughput

Receiver tests pass on ≥ 95 % of maximum throughput. Because block errors are random, the test must run long enough that the decision is statistically sound; 3GPP specifies the number of slots or uses early-decision statistics (pass or fail as soon as a confidence bound clears the limit) to keep test time manageable. Fading tests need durations spanning many coherence times.

Over-the-air (OTA) testing

Conducted tests exclude the antenna, but users experience radiated performance. Operators and certification bodies therefore require OTA metrics, and in FR2, where antenna arrays are integrated with the RF chips, OTA is the only way to test at all.

FR1: TRP and TIS

The UE is rotated in an anechoic chamber (combined-axis or distributed-axis positioner) and EIRP or EIS is measured on a spherical grid in both polarisations, typically 15° for TRP and 30° for TIS. Test conditions include free space, beside head and hand (left/right) with SAM head and hand phantoms, and hand only for data devices.

TRP ≈ π/(2NM) · Σᵢ Σⱼ [EIRP_θ(θᵢ,φⱼ) + EIRP_φ(θᵢ,φⱼ)] sin θᵢ     TIS ≈ 2NM / {π Σᵢ Σⱼ [1/EIS_θ + 1/EIS_φ] sin θᵢ}

TRP is a power average; TIS is a harmonic mean of sensitivity, dominated by the best directions. Antenna efficiency, hand detuning and the tuner state dominate the difference between conducted and radiated numbers. MIMO OTA (throughput in an emulated spatial channel) uses multi-probe anechoic, radiated two-stage or reverberation-chamber methods.

General procedure for a conducted test

  1. Prepare. Warm up and self-calibrate the instruments. Measure the loss of every cable and combiner at the test frequencies and enter it as a correction.
  2. Connect. Cable the phone's antenna port (or RF test connector) to the tester inside the shield box. Fit the test SIM. Power the phone from the programmable supply at nominal voltage.
  3. Configure the cell. Set band, channel (low, mid and high are each tested), bandwidth, subcarrier spacing and downlink level as TS 38.508-1 and the test case specify.
  4. Attach and set up the call. Let the phone register, then establish a connection with the reference measurement channel for the test: a fixed modulation, coding and resource-block allocation.
  5. Set the test condition. For example, send continuous "power up" commands for maximum-power tests, or set the downlink to the sensitivity level and add interferers for receiver tests.
  6. Measure. Average over the specified number of slots or subframes. For receiver tests, count acknowledged blocks until the result is statistically conclusive.
  7. Compare. Apply the limit plus the allowed test tolerance from the conformance specification. Record pass or fail with the margin.
  8. Repeat. Step through channels, bandwidths, allocations, modulations, and extreme temperature and voltage. Automation makes this practical.

Worked examples

Example: maximum output power, EVM and ACLR in one set-up

  1. Connect on Band n78, mid channel, 100 MHz, 30 kHz spacing. Uplink: DFT-s-OFDM, QPSK, full allocation.
  2. Command power "all up" and wait for the power to settle.
  3. Read mean channel power. Expect 23 dBm ± 2 dB plus tolerance.
  4. Without changing anything, read ACLR on both sides. Expect at least 30 dB.
  5. Read EVM. Expect below 17.5 % for QPSK.
  6. Switch the uplink to 256QAM. Power may drop by the permitted MPR. Read EVM again; expect below 3.5 %.
  7. Repeat with edge allocations, where emission limits are hardest to meet, then on low and high channels.

Example: reference sensitivity

  1. Connect with the downlink reference channel (QPSK, rate 1/3, full allocation) and the uplink at maximum power in the allocation the specification defines, so transmit leakage into the receiver is at its worst.
  2. Set the downlink power to the reference sensitivity level for the band and bandwidth, corrected for cable loss.
  3. Measure throughput over enough blocks for a confident verdict.
  4. Pass if throughput is at least 95 % of the maximum.
  5. For engineering work, continue stepping the level down in 0.5 dB steps to find the actual sensitivity and the margin to the limit.

Production test and calibration

Production test must guarantee that every shipped unit meets the specification in seconds per band, not hours. It relies on design verification having established margin, and on calibration to remove part-to-part variation.

Typical flow

  1. Board test after SMT: power rails, digital interfaces, basic RF continuity.
  2. RF calibration in non-signalling (factory test) mode, driven over a diagnostic interface with list-mode sequencing on the tester.
  3. RF verification: a reduced set of power, EVM, ACLR, frequency error and RX level checks at representative channels, often several DUTs in parallel per tester.
  4. Final assembly test: radiated or coupler-based check that each antenna path is connected and efficient (catches missing springs and connectors).

Limits and statistics

  • Guard-banding: production limits = specification limits tightened by the test system's expanded uncertainty, so that a passing unit is compliant with high confidence.
  • Process capability: C_pk = min(USL − μ, μ − LSL)/(3σ); ≥ 1.33 is a common target, ≥ 1.67 for safety-related or regulatory parameters.
  • Correlation: golden units measured in the lab and on each production tester keep stations aligned; drift triggers recalibration of test fixtures.
  • Test-time reduction: drop tests whose distributions sit far from limits (high Cpk) to periodic sampling; keep tests that catch assembly defects.
