Alternating Current

Series LCR circuit, resonance, power factor and the transformer

Mains electricity is an alternating voltage. In an ac circuit a resistor, inductor and capacitor each respond differently, so the current is generally out of step with the voltage. At one special frequency, resonance, the inductor and capacitor cancel and the current is largest.

  • rms values: Vrms = V₀/√2. Indian mains is 230 V rms at 50 Hz, a peak of about 325 V.
  • Reactances XL = ωL grows with frequency, XC = 1/(ωC) falls. The impedance Z = √(R² + (XL − XC)²).
  • At resonance ω₀ = 1/√(LC): XL = XC, Z = R, current is maximum and in phase with the voltage. A sharper peak means a higher Q = ω₀L/R.
  • Average power P = Vrms Irms cosφ, where cosφ = R/Z is the power factor. A pure L or C absorbs no average power.
  • A transformer changes ac voltage using mutual induction: Vs/Vp = Ns/Np, and (ideally) VpIp = VsIs.

In the real world

Radio tuning is resonance. To receive an FM station at 100 MHz with a coil of L = 1 µH, the tuning capacitor must be C = 1/(ω²L) with ω = 2π × 10⁸ = 6.28×10⁸ rad/s, so C = 1/(3.95×10¹⁷ × 10⁻⁶) ≈ 2.5 pF. Turning the knob of an old radio changes C so that f₀ = 1/(2π√LC) matches the station and the circuit responds strongly only in a narrow band around it.

At an Indian 11 kV/415 V distribution substation, a 100 kVA transformer has Ns/Np ≈ 415/11000. Ideally it supplies Is = 100000/415 ≈ 241 A while drawing only Ip = 100000/11000 ≈ 9.1 A from the line. High-voltage transmission keeps the current small and therefore the I²R loss small. Power factor matters to industry for the same reason: a motor with cosφ = 0.7 needs 1/0.7 ≈ 1.43 times more current than at cosφ = 1 for the same real power, so utilities penalise low power factor and factories add capacitor banks.

In a series LCR circuit driven exactly at its resonant frequency, which is true?

At ω₀ the reactances XL and XC are equal and cancel, so Z = R (its minimum), I = V/R is the maximum, φ = 0 and cosφ = 1. The voltages across L and C are individually large (Q times the supply voltage) but opposite in phase.
