Random variables and the binomial distribution

A random variable X assigns a number to each outcome of an experiment, such as the number of boundaries in an over. Its probability distribution lists each value with its probability, and the mean (expected value) and variance summarise where the values sit and how spread out they are. When you repeat the same yes/no trial n times independently, the count of successes follows the binomial distribution.

  • A discrete random variable has a distribution {xi, pi} with every pi ≥ 0 and Σpi = 1.
  • Mean E(X) = Σ xipi. Variance Var(X) = Σ xi²pi − [E(X)]². Standard deviation σ = √Var(X).
  • Bernoulli trials: a fixed number n of trials, each with only two outcomes (success or failure), the same success probability p each time, and independent trials.
  • For X ~ B(n, p): P(X = r) = nCr pr qn−r, with q = 1 − p, mean np and variance npq.
  • The distribution is symmetric when p = ½, skewed right when p < ½ and skewed left when p > ½.

In the real world

A batter hits a boundary on 20% of the balls he faces, and balls are treated as independent trials. The number of boundaries in an over of 6 balls is X ~ B(6, 0.2). Then P(X = 2) = 6C2 (0.2)² (0.8)⁴ = 15 × 0.04 × 0.4096 = 0.24576, about 24.6%. The mean is np = 6 × 0.2 = 1.2 boundaries per over and the variance is npq = 6 × 0.2 × 0.8 = 0.96. The chance of at least one boundary is 1 − 0.8⁶ = 1 − 0.262144 = 0.737856, about 73.8%.

Over a 50-over innings (300 balls) the mean is 300 × 0.2 = 60 boundaries with standard deviation √(300 × 0.2 × 0.8) = √48 ≈ 6.9. The same model is used for defective bulbs in a batch, patients who respond to a drug, or votes cast for one candidate in a sample of voters.
