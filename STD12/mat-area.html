Area under curves and between curves

The definite integral already measures area under a curve. To find the area trapped between two curves, integrate the gap between them: upper curve minus lower curve, over the interval where they enclose the region. The limits come from where the curves meet.

  • Area under y = f(x) from x = a to b (f ≥ 0) is ∫ab f(x)dx. Where f is negative, take the absolute value of that piece.
  • Between two curves with f(x) ≥ g(x): Area = ∫ab [f(x) − g(x)]dx.
  • First solve f(x) = g(x) for the points of intersection; these usually give a and b.
  • Sketch the region first. Use symmetry (circle, ellipse, even functions) to compute a quarter or half and multiply.
  • Sometimes it is easier to integrate along y: Area = ∫ x dy.

In the real world

A surveyor must find the area of a field plot that has a straight road on one side (the x-axis, 100 m long) and a curved canal bank on the other, modelled by y = 0.01x(100 − x), which bulges 25 m at x = 50. Area = ∫₀¹⁰⁰ 0.01(100x − x²)dx = 0.01[50x² − x³/3]₀¹⁰⁰ = 0.01(500000 − 333333.33) = 1666.67 m², about 0.167 hectare. Check by Simpson's rule with ordinates 0, 18.75, 25, 18.75, 0 at spacing 25 m: (25/3)(0 + 4×18.75 + 2×25 + 4×18.75 + 0) = (25/3)(200) = 1666.67 m². The two methods agree because Simpson's rule is exact for parabolas.

The curves meet where x² = x, at x = 0 and 1. On (0, 1) the line is above the parabola, so the area is ∫₀¹ (x − x²)dx = 1/2 − 1/3 = 1/6.
