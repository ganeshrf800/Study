Inverse Trigonometric Functions

Principal value branches and key identities

Sine, cosine and the rest repeat forever, so none of them is one-one on R. To define an inverse we keep one stretch where the function is one-one and covers its whole range: the principal value branch. Each inverse trigonometric function then returns a single, agreed angle.

  • sin⁻¹x is the angle in [−π/2, π/2] whose sine is x; cos⁻¹x lies in [0, π]; tan⁻¹x lies in (−π/2, π/2).
  • cot⁻¹x lies in (0, π); sec⁻¹x lies in [0, π] except π/2; cosec⁻¹x lies in [−π/2, π/2] except 0.
  • sin⁻¹x is not 1/sin x. The −1 is a notation for the inverse function, not a power.
  • sin(sin⁻¹x) = x for x in [−1, 1], but sin⁻¹(sin x) = x only when x is in [−π/2, π/2].
  • Every inverse graph is the mirror image of the restricted parent graph in y = x.

In the real world

The Qutub Minar in Delhi is about 72.5 m tall. A visitor standing 100 m from its base sees the top at an angle of elevation θ with tan θ = 72.5/100 = 0.725, so θ = tan⁻¹(0.725) ≈ 35.94°. Surveyors, camera lenses and robot arms all use inverse trigonometry to turn measured lengths back into angles. Moving to 50 m away gives tan⁻¹(1.45) ≈ 55.4°.

What is the principal value of sin⁻¹(sin 2π/3)?

2π/3 is outside [−π/2, π/2]. Since sin(2π/3) = sin(π − 2π/3) = sin(π/3) and π/3 lies in the principal range, the answer is π/3.
