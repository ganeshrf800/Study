Bohr model of hydrogen and spectral series

Rutherford's nuclear atom could not explain why atoms give out light only at certain sharp wavelengths. Bohr proposed that the electron moves only in special orbits with fixed energies, and emits a photon when it jumps down from one orbit to another. Each jump gives one line in the hydrogen spectrum.

  • Bohr's postulates: electrons orbit without radiating in allowed orbits where angular momentum mvr = nh/2π; a photon of energy hν = Ei − Ef is emitted or absorbed in a jump.
  • Energy of the n-th level of hydrogen: En = −13.6/n² eV. The negative sign means the electron is bound; n = ∞ is 0 eV (ionised).
  • Orbit radius grows as n² and orbital speed falls as 1/n.
  • Series: Lyman (to n = 1, ultraviolet), Balmer (to n = 2, visible), Paschen (to n = 3, infrared), Brackett (n = 4) and Pfund (n = 5), both infrared.

In the real world

Astronomers identify hydrogen in stars and nebulae by its Balmer lines. The first Balmer line, H-alpha, comes from the jump n = 3 → 2: ΔE = 13.6 (1/4 − 1/9) = 13.6 × 0.1389 = 1.89 eV, so λ = 1240/1.89 = 656 nm, a deep red. India's Kodaikanal Solar Observatory has photographed the Sun in this line for over a century, tracing sunspots and flares. Lyman-alpha (2 → 1) has ΔE = 13.6 × 0.75 = 10.2 eV and λ = 121.6 nm, in the ultraviolet, so ground telescopes never see it and space instruments are needed. Ionisation energy: raising the electron from n = 1 to n = ∞ costs 13.6 eV, the minimum energy of a photon that can strip hydrogen.

Which transition of hydrogen gives a line in the visible region?

The Balmer series ends at n = 2 and has visible lines (656, 486, 434, 410 nm). Transitions to n = 1 are Lyman (ultraviolet) and to n = 3 are Paschen (infrared). For 4 → 2: ΔE = 13.6 (1/4 − 1/16) = 2.55 eV, λ = 486 nm, blue-green.
