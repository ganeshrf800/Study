Haloalkanes and Haloarenes (NCERT Chemistry Part II, Ch 6)

Nucleophilic substitution: SN1 versus SN2

In a haloalkane the carbon bonded to the halogen is electron-poor, so nucleophiles attack it and push the halide out. Whether this happens in one concerted step (SN2) or in two steps through a carbocation (SN1) depends on the substrate, the nucleophile and the solvent.

  • SN2: one step, backside attack, rate = k[R–X][Nu], inversion of configuration (Walden inversion). Fastest for methyl and primary halides because the carbon is least crowded.
  • SN1: slow ionisation to a planar carbocation, then fast attack by the nucleophile. Rate = k[R–X], racemisation. Fastest for tertiary halides (most stable carbocation) and in polar protic solvents.
  • Leaving-group ability: I⁻ > Br⁻ > Cl⁻ > F⁻, so reactivity R–I > R–Br > R–Cl > R–F, even though C–F is the most polar bond.
  • Haloarenes resist substitution: the C–X carbon is sp² and the lone pair of X is delocalised into the ring, giving the C–X bond partial double-bond character. Chlorobenzene needs about 623 K and 300 atm with NaOH; an o/p nitro group lowers this to about 443 K, two nitro groups to 368 K, and three (2,4,6-trinitrochlorobenzene) allow reaction with water at room temperature.
  • With strong bases such as alcoholic KOH, elimination (dehydrohalogenation, Saytzeff rule) competes with substitution, especially for 2° and 3° halides.

In the real world

Chlorofluorocarbons were made by the Swarts reaction (R–Cl + SbF3) and were phased out under the Montreal Protocol because their C–Cl bonds break in the stratosphere. For a rate-law check: for CH3Br + OH⁻ (SN2), doubling both [CH3Br] and [OH⁻] raises the rate 2 × 2 = 4 times. For (CH3)3CBr in aqueous solution (SN1), doubling [OH⁻] changes nothing and doubling [(CH3)3CBr] doubles the rate. This is the experimental way chemists tell the two mechanisms apart. Chloramphenicol, an antibiotic, and the antiseptic chloroxylenol (Dettol) are organohalogen compounds in everyday Indian medicine cabinets.

Optically active (R)-2-bromobutane reacts with OH⁻ in acetone and gives (S)-butan-2-ol almost exclusively. Which mechanism is this?

A strong nucleophile in an aprotic solvent on a secondary halide gives SN2. Backside attack inverts the stereocentre (R to S here). SN1 would give a racemic mixture because the carbocation is planar.
