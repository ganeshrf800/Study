Electromagnetic Waves

Electromagnetic waves: spectrum, E and B oscillation, speed c

A changing electric field produces a magnetic field, and a changing magnetic field produces an electric field. Together they can sustain each other and travel through empty space as an electromagnetic wave. Radio, microwaves, light and X-rays are the same kind of wave at different frequencies.

  • Maxwell's displacement current, Id = ε₀ dΦE/dt, completes Ampere's law and predicts the waves.
  • E and B are perpendicular to each other and to the direction of travel (transverse), oscillate in phase, and E₀/B₀ = c.
  • In vacuum c = 1/√(μ₀ε₀) ≈ 3×10⁸ m/s for all frequencies. In a medium the speed is lower.
  • The spectrum in order of increasing frequency: radio, microwave, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X-ray, gamma. Higher frequency means shorter wavelength and larger photon energy.
  • The wave carries energy: average energy density u = ½ε₀E₀², intensity I = ½ε₀cE₀².

In the real world

A microwave oven works at 2.45 GHz, so λ = c/f = 3×10⁸ / 2.45×10⁹ ≈ 0.122 m = 12.2 cm. Inside the cavity the waves form a standing pattern with hot spots half a wavelength (about 6.1 cm) apart, which is why a turntable is used and why a chocolate bar left still melts in separated patches. Indian 4G networks use bands such as 850, 1800, 2100 and 2300 MHz, while 5G uses the 3.3 to 3.67 GHz mid-band (λ ≈ 8.6 cm at 3.5 GHz) and a 26 GHz millimetre-wave band (λ ≈ 1.15 cm). A 3.5 GHz photon has energy hf = 6.626×10⁻³⁴ × 3.5×10⁹ ≈ 2.3×10⁻²⁴ J ≈ 1.4×10⁻⁵ eV, far below the few eV needed to break chemical bonds, so these waves heat but cannot ionise.

Sunlight at Earth carries roughly 1000 W/m². Using I = ½ε₀cE₀², E₀ = √(2I/(ε₀c)) = √(2000 / (8.85×10⁻¹² × 3×10⁸)) ≈ 870 V/m, and B₀ = E₀/c ≈ 2.9×10⁻⁶ T.

For a plane electromagnetic wave travelling in vacuum, which statement is correct?

EM waves are transverse. E and B oscillate in phase, with E₀ = cB₀ and the direction of travel along E × B. All frequencies travel at the same speed c = 1/√(μ₀ε₀) in vacuum.
