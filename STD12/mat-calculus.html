Continuity and Differentiability

Limits, continuity and the derivative as a slope

A function is continuous at a point if you can draw it through that point without lifting the pen: the limit exists and equals the value. It is differentiable there if, on zooming in far enough, the curve looks like a straight line, and the slope of that line is the derivative.

  • f is continuous at a when LHL = RHL = f(a), that is lim f(x) as x → a equals f(a).
  • The derivative is the limit of the difference quotient: f′(a) = limh→0 [f(a+h) − f(a)]/h. It exists only if the left and right limits agree.
  • Differentiable implies continuous, but not the other way round: |x| is continuous at 0 and has a corner there, so no derivative.
  • The chain rule differentiates a composite from outside to inside: d/dx f(g(x)) = f′(g(x))·g′(x).
  • Rolle's theorem and the Mean Value Theorem guarantee a point where the tangent is parallel to the chord.

In the real world

A Delhi Metro train leaves a platform with position s = 2t² + 5t metres after t seconds. Its instantaneous speed at t = 3 s is the derivative s′(t) = 4t + 5, which at t = 3 is 17 m/s. Check with the first-principles quotient and h = 0.01 s: s(3.01) = 2(9.0601) + 15.05 = 33.1702 m and s(3) = 33 m, so the average speed over that interval is 0.1702/0.01 = 17.02 m/s, already close to 17. A speedometer is a device that estimates this limit.
