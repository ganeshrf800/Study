Chemical Kinetics (NCERT Chemistry Part I, Ch 3)

Rate law, order, half-life and the Arrhenius equation

Kinetics asks how fast a reaction goes. The rate law links rate to concentrations through an order found by experiment, and each order has its own integrated equation and half-life. Temperature matters hugely: the Arrhenius equation shows that the rate constant grows exponentially as the activation energy barrier becomes easier to cross.

  • Rate = k[A]x. The order x is found from experiments and need not match the coefficient in the balanced equation.
  • Zero order: [A] falls linearly. First order: [A] decays exponentially and t½ is independent of [A]0. Second order: t½ ∝ 1/[A]0.
  • Only molecules with energy at least Ea react. Raising T raises that fraction sharply; a catalyst lowers Ea.
  • For many reactions near room temperature, a 10 K rise roughly doubles or triples the rate.

In the real world

Pressure cooker. Inside a cooker the pressure is about 1.5 atm, so water boils near 120 °C instead of 100 °C. With the rule that each 10 K doubles the rate, 20 K gives 2² = 4 times faster reaction, so dal that needs 60 minutes in an open pan is ready in about 15 minutes. (The rule corresponds to Ea ≈ 53.6 kJ/mol near 300 K: 2.303 × 8.314 × 0.301 × (300 × 310 / 10) ≈ 53.6 × 10³ J.)

Fridge preserving food. Going from 27 °C to 4 °C is a 23 K drop, so spoilage reactions slow by roughly 22.3 ≈ 5 times. Milk that turns sour in about 6 hours at room temperature lasts more than a day in the fridge.

A first-order reaction has k = 0.0231 min⁻¹. What is its half-life?

t½ = 0.693 / k = 0.693 / 0.0231 = 30 min. For a first-order reaction the half-life does not depend on the initial concentration.
