Electromagnetic Induction

Faraday's law, Lenz's law, motional emf and eddy currents

A changing magnetic flux through a loop creates an emf in it. The faster the flux changes, the larger the emf, and the induced current always flows so as to oppose the change that caused it. This is the principle behind generators, transformers, induction cooktops and regenerative brakes.

  • Magnetic flux Φ = BA cosθ. An emf appears whenever B, A or θ changes with time.
  • Faraday: ε = −N dΦ/dt. The minus sign is Lenz's law: the induced current opposes the change in flux (conservation of energy).
  • A conductor of length l moving at speed v across a field B develops motional emf ε = Blv.
  • Eddy currents are induced loops of current in bulk metal. They heat the metal (induction cooktop) or produce braking force (magnetic brakes).

In the real world

Take B = 0.5 T, l = 0.4 m, v = 5 m/s and R = 2 Ω. Then ε = Blv = 0.5 × 0.4 × 5 = 1.0 V, I = 1.0/2 = 0.5 A, and the magnetic drag is F = BIl = 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.4 = 0.1 N. The power you must supply is Fv = 0.1 × 5 = 0.5 W, exactly equal to I²R = 0.25 × 2 = 0.5 W dissipated as heat. The energy comes from whoever pushes the rod, which is Lenz's law in energy terms.

An induction cooktop runs an alternating current of tens of kilohertz in a coil under the glass. The changing flux drives eddy currents in the steel base of the pan, which heats directly while the glass stays cool; that is why aluminium-free, magnetic steel or iron utensils work best. Metro trains such as the Delhi Metro use the reverse: during braking the traction motors act as generators, the train's kinetic energy becomes electrical energy and a good part of it is fed back into the overhead line for other trains to use.
