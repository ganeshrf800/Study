Integrals

The definite integral as area, and the fundamental theorem

Slice the region under a curve into thin strips, add up their areas, and let the strips get thinner. The sum settles down to a number: the definite integral. The fundamental theorem of calculus says this limit can be found without any slicing, by evaluating an antiderivative at the two ends.

  • An indefinite integral ∫f(x)dx = F(x) + C is the family of antiderivatives, F′ = f.
  • The definite integral ∫ab f(x)dx is the limit of Riemann sums; for f ≥ 0 it is the area under the curve; below the x-axis the area counts negative.
  • First fundamental theorem: if A(x) = ∫ax f(t)dt, then A′(x) = f(x). Second: ∫ab f(x)dx = F(b) − F(a).
  • Methods: substitution, integration by parts, partial fractions, and standard forms.
  • Useful property: ∫0a f(x)dx = ∫0a f(a − x)dx, and an odd function over [−a, a] integrates to 0.

In the real world

A rocket's accelerometer records acceleration, and the flight computer integrates it to get velocity. Take an illustrative first-stage profile for an ISRO launch vehicle (not an actual flight record): a(t) = 10 + 0.1t m/s² for 0 ≤ t ≤ 60 s. Then v(60) = ∫₀⁶⁰ (10 + 0.1t)dt = [10t + 0.05t²]₀⁶⁰ = 600 + 180 = 780 m/s. Integrating again, the distance covered is ∫₀⁶⁰ (10t + 0.05t²)dt = [5t² + 0.05t³/3]₀⁶⁰ = 18000 + 3600 = 21600 m, that is 21.6 km. (Real vehicles also lose mass and fight gravity and drag, so the real acceleration is not this simple.)

What is ∫₀^π sin x dx?

∫ sin x dx = −cos x, so the value is −cos π − (−cos 0) = 1 + 1 = 2. This is the area under one hump of the sine curve, and the Riemann sum above converges to it.
