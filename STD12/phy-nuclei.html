Nuclei

Radioactive decay, half-life and binding energy

Unstable nuclei decay at random, yet a large sample decays in a perfectly predictable way: the same fraction disappears in each equal interval of time. The energy that holds a nucleus together, its binding energy, explains why fusion of light nuclei and fission of heavy ones both release energy.

  • Decay is random for a single nucleus, but the number left follows N = N0 e-λt for a large sample. The decay constant λ is the probability of decay per unit time.
  • Half-life T1/2 = 0.693/λ is the time for half the nuclei to decay. Mean life τ = 1/λ.
  • Activity A = λN is the number of decays per second (becquerel, Bq). It falls in the same way as N.
  • Mass defect Δm = Zmp + (A − Z)mn − mnucleus; binding energy = Δm c², and 1 u = 931.5 MeV.
  • Binding energy per nucleon rises to a peak of about 8.8 MeV near iron (A = 56): fusing light nuclei or splitting heavy ones moves towards the peak and releases energy.

In the real world

Carbon dating. Living wood holds carbon-14 at a fixed ratio to carbon-12; after death the C-14 (T1/2 = 5730 years) decays and is not replaced. If a charcoal sample from an archaeological site such as Lothal or Dholavira has only 25% of the original C-14 activity, then N/N0 = (½)t/5730 = ¼ gives t = 2 half-lives = 11,460 years. Nuclear power: Tarapur (India's first reactors, 1969) and Kudankulam (two VVER-1000 units, 1000 MW each) use fission. Fissioning U-235 (B.E./A ≈ 7.6 MeV) into fragments near A ≈ 118 (≈ 8.5 MeV) gains about 0.9 × 236 ≈ 200 MeV per nucleus. One kilogram of U-235 holds 6.022×1023 × 1000/235 = 2.56×1024 nuclei, releasing 2.56×1024 × 200 × 1.6×10-13 J ≈ 8.2×1013 J, about 2.3×107 kWh (23 million units).

A radioactive sample has a half-life of 10 days. What fraction of the original nuclei remains after 30 days?

30 days is 3 half-lives, so N/N0 = (½)³ = 1/8.
