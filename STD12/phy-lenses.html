Ray Optics and Optical Instruments

Lenses, mirrors and optical instruments

A lens bends light by refraction and a mirror turns it back by reflection, and in both cases rays from one point of an object can meet again to form an image. The same few formulae give the position, size and nature of the image, and combining lenses gives microscopes and telescopes.

  • Use the Cartesian sign convention: distances are measured from the pole or optical centre, positive along the direction of incident light. Object distance u is negative for a real object.
  • Lens: 1/v − 1/u = 1/f. Mirror: 1/v + 1/u = 1/f. A convex lens and a concave mirror are converging (they can form real images).
  • Magnification m = hi/ho: for a lens m = v/u, for a mirror m = −v/u. Negative m means inverted.
  • Power of a lens P = 1/f with f in metres, in dioptre (D). Powers of thin lenses in contact add.
  • The lens maker's formula gives f from the refractive index and the radii of curvature of the two surfaces.

In the real world

Spectacles. A myopic student whose far point is 50 cm cannot see distant boards clearly. A concave lens must form a virtual image of a distant object (u = ∞) at 50 cm, so v = −50 cm and f = −50 cm. The power is P = 100/(−50) = −2.0 D, the "−2.00" printed on the prescription. Cameras use the same thin lens formula: a phone lens of f = 5 mm focuses a subject 1 m away where 1/v = 1/f + 1/u = 1/0.5 − 1/100 = 1.99 cm⁻¹, so v = 0.5025 cm, only 0.025 cm beyond the focal plane; autofocus just moves the lens by that sliver. Worked check: an object 15 cm from a convex lens of f = 10 cm gives 1/v = 1/10 − 1/15 = 1/30, so v = 30 cm and m = v/u = −2, a real inverted image twice as large, as in a projector.
