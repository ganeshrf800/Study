Evolution

Darwin, natural selection and the evidence for evolution

Populations carry heritable variation. Individuals whose traits suit the environment leave more offspring, so those traits become more common in the next generation. Darwin called this natural selection, and over long times it builds new adaptations and new species.

  • Darwin's argument: variation exists; more offspring are born than can survive (struggle for existence); the fitter survive and reproduce (survival of the fittest); useful variations accumulate. Lamarck's use and disuse (giraffe's neck) is not inherited. De Vries proposed saltation by large mutations.
  • Evidence: fossils (Archaeopteryx links reptiles and birds); comparative anatomy; embryology (vertebrate embryos look alike early); biochemical similarity (same genetic code, cytochrome c); biogeography; and observed natural selection.
  • Homologous organs (same structure, different function) show divergent evolution: forelimbs of human, whale, bat and cheetah. Analogous organs (different structure, same function) show convergent evolution: wings of insect and bird, flippers of penguin and dolphin.
  • Adaptive radiation: one ancestor spreads into many niches, as in Darwin's finches on Galápagos and Australian marsupials.
  • Origin of life: Oparin and Haldane proposed chemical evolution in a reducing atmosphere; Miller (1953) made amino acids from CH4, NH3, H2 and water vapour with electric sparks. Human lineage: Homo habilis (about 650 to 800 cc brain), H. erectus (about 900 cc), Neanderthal (about 1400 cc) and H. sapiens (about 1350 to 1400 cc).

In the real world

Antibiotic resistance is natural selection at fast-forward. Suppose 1 in 107 bacteria carries a resistance mutation. In an infection of 109 cells that is 109 / 107 = 100 resistant cells. The antibiotic kills the other cells, the 100 survivors divide every 20 minutes or so, and in 24 hours (72 generations) they refill the body. Skipping doses or stopping early selects exactly these survivors. New Delhi metallo-β-lactamase 1 (NDM-1), first described in 2008 in a patient treated in India and Sweden, spread worldwide on plasmids, and India's Chennai Declaration (2012) and National Action Plan on Antimicrobial Resistance (2017) aim to limit misuse. In the moth widget, dark moths reached about 98% of the Manchester population within roughly 50 years of the first dark specimen (1848).
