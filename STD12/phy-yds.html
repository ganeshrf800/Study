Wave Optics

Interference and Young's double slit experiment

Light from two narrow slits overlaps on a screen. Where the waves arrive in step they add and make a bright fringe; where they arrive half a wavelength apart they cancel and leave a dark fringe. The evenly spaced stripes are direct proof that light is a wave.

  • Interference needs coherent sources: a constant phase difference, which is why both slits are lit by the same wavefront.
  • Path difference Δ = d sin θ ≈ d y / D for small angles. Bright fringe when Δ = nλ, dark when Δ = (n + ½)λ.
  • Fringes are equally spaced with width β = λD/d. Red light (larger λ) gives wider fringes than blue.
  • Intensity varies as I = 4I0 cos²(φ/2), where the phase difference φ = 2πΔ/λ. The maximum is 4I0, not 2I0: energy is redistributed, not created.

In the real world

Interference is why spectacle and camera lenses have a faint purple-green tint. An anti-reflection coating of magnesium fluoride (n = 1.38) is made one quarter-wavelength thick, so light reflected from its top and bottom surfaces is half a wavelength out of step and cancels. For λ = 550 nm (green, where the eye is most sensitive) the thickness is t = λ/(4n) = 550/(4 × 1.38) = 99.6 nm ≈ 100 nm. The same idea gives the rainbow colours on a soap film or a CD. For the double slit itself, take sodium light λ = 589 nm, d = 1 mm, D = 1 m: β = λD/d = 589×10-9 × 1 / 10-3 = 0.589 mm, so about 17 bright fringes fit in 1 cm of screen, close enough to count with a magnifier.

In Young's experiment the slit separation d is halved and the screen distance D is doubled. The fringe width becomes

β = λD/d. Halving d doubles β and doubling D doubles it again, so β becomes 4 times larger.
