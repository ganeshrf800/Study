Electric Charges and Fields

Gauss's law: flux and symmetric charge distributions

Electric flux counts how much field passes through a surface. Gauss's law says the flux through any closed surface depends only on the charge inside it. For charge distributions with enough symmetry, this gives the field in a few lines, with no integration.

  • Flux through a flat area is Φ = E A cosθ, where θ is the angle between E and the area's normal. It is a scalar, and it is zero when the surface is parallel to the field.
  • Gauss's law: the net flux through a closed surface is qenc/ε₀. Charges outside contribute zero net flux.
  • Choose a Gaussian surface on which E is constant (or zero) and perpendicular to the surface: a sphere, a coaxial cylinder or a pillbox.
  • Inside a conductor's cavity or a charged shell, the field is zero; a charged conductor keeps all its charge on the outer surface.
  • Field falls as 1/r² for a sphere, 1/r for a long line, and does not fall at all for an infinite sheet.

In the real world

Fair-weather Earth has a downward field of about 120 N/C at the surface, so the ground carries negative charge. Treating the surface as a conductor, E = σ/ε₀ gives σ = 8.85×10⁻¹² × 120 ≈ 1.06×10⁻⁹ C m⁻². Over the Earth's area 4π(6.37×10⁶)² ≈ 5.1×10¹⁴ m², the total charge is about 5.4×10⁵ C (negative).

Gauss's law also explains why a car or a bus is a safe place in a lightning storm: it acts as a hollow conductor, so the field inside is zero and the charge stays on the outer metal skin. Sensitive electronics are shielded the same way in metal cabinets (Faraday cages).
