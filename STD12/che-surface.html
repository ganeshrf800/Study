Surface Chemistry (NCERT Chemistry Part II, older editions)

Adsorption, catalysis and colloids

Atoms at a surface have unbalanced forces, so surfaces attract and hold molecules of gases or dissolved substances. This is adsorption, and it underlies filters, catalysts and the behaviour of colloids, mixtures whose particles are 1 to 1000 nm across.

  • Adsorption is a surface phenomenon (absorption is throughout the bulk). Physisorption is weak, reversible, exothermic and falls with rising temperature; chemisorption is strong, specific and forms bonds.
  • Freundlich isotherm: x/m = kP1/n, an empirical power law. Langmuir isotherm: monolayer coverage θ = KP/(1 + KP), which saturates at high pressure.
  • A catalyst changes the rate by giving a path with lower activation energy and is unchanged at the end. Enzymes are highly specific biological catalysts.
  • Colloids scatter light (Tyndall effect), show Brownian motion, and carry charge. Electrolytes can coagulate them (Hardy-Schulze rule). Types include sol, gel, emulsion and aerosol.

In the real world

Activated charcoal water filters. Activated carbon has an internal surface of around 1000 m² per gram. A 10 g cartridge therefore offers about 10,000 m², roughly 1.4 football fields (7140 m² each), on which chlorine, dissolved organics and bad taste molecules stick by physisorption. It saturates like a Langmuir surface, which is why cartridges must be replaced.

Milk and rubber as colloids. Amul milk is an emulsion: fat globules of roughly 1 to 10 micrometres, stabilised by casein, dispersed in water. Adding lactic acid (curd bacteria) or lemon juice coagulates the casein. Natural rubber latex from trees in Kerala is also a colloid; adding acid coagulates it into rubber sheets for balls and tyres.

In the Langmuir isotherm, what happens to the amount adsorbed at very high pressure?

At high P, KP ≫ 1, so θ = KP/(1 + KP) → 1: every site is occupied and further pressure adds nothing. The unbounded power law is the empirical Freundlich form.
