Refraction, Snell's law and total internal reflection

When light crosses from one transparent medium into another its speed changes, so the ray bends at the boundary. Going from a denser to a rarer medium, the ray bends away from the normal, and beyond a certain angle it cannot leave at all: it is reflected completely back.

  • Refractive index n = c/v. A higher n means slower light and a ray that bends towards the normal on entering that medium.
  • Snell's law: n1 sin i = n2 sin r, with both angles measured from the normal.
  • Total internal reflection (TIR) needs light travelling from denser to rarer medium with incidence angle greater than the critical angle θc.
  • At TIR no energy is lost to the second medium, which is why optical fibres, prisms in binoculars and diamonds work so well.

In the real world

Most internet traffic into Indian homes and mobile towers now rides on optical fibre (BharatNet is laying it to gram panchayats). Light is guided inside a glass core of n = 1.48 surrounded by cladding of n = 1.46. The critical angle is sin-1(1.46/1.48) = 80.6°, so any ray hitting the core wall at more than 80.6° to the normal is totally reflected, kilometre after kilometre, with almost no loss. The acceptance angle follows from NA = √(1.48² − 1.46²) = √0.0588 = 0.243, i.e. a cone of half-angle sin-1(0.243) ≈ 14° at the fibre end. Try the "Fibre core to cladding" preset and push i past 80.6°. A diamond (n = 2.42) has θc = sin-1(1/2.42) = 24.4°, so light entering it gets trapped and bounces many times, giving its sparkle.

Light travels from water (n = 1.33) into air. What is the critical angle?

sin θc = n2/n1 = 1/1.33 = 0.752, so θc = 48.8°. For angles of incidence above this, light in water is totally reflected at the surface.
