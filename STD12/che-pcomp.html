The p-Block Elements (NCERT Chemistry Part II, Ch 7)

Four industrial and environmental p-block compounds

Ammonia, sulphuric acid, ozone and chlorine are made, used or regulated on a vast scale. The first two are made by reversible, exothermic gas reactions where Le Chatelier's principle decides the plant conditions; ozone and chlorine are powerful oxidising agents that protect or purify, and harm when mishandled.

  • Haber process: N2 + 3H2 ⇌ 2NH3, ΔH = −92.4 kJ mol⁻¹. High pressure (about 200 atm) favours NH3; low temperature favours it too but is too slow, so about 700 K with an iron catalyst (K2O and Al2O3 promoters) is a compromise.
  • Contact process: 2SO2 + O2 ⇌ 2SO3, ΔH = −196 kJ mol⁻¹, V2O5 catalyst, about 720 K and only about 2 bar because the yield is already high. SO3 is absorbed in conc. H2SO4 to give oleum (H2S2O7), which is then diluted.
  • Ozone, O3: bent molecule (about 117°), made by a silent electric discharge through O2, endothermic and unstable, a strong oxidant. The stratospheric ozone layer absorbs harmful UV; CFCs destroy it.
  • Chlorine: made by electrolysis of brine (chlor-alkali) or the Deacon process. Moist Cl2 bleaches by oxidation through HOCl, and kills microbes in drinking water.

In the real world

Worked example: ammonia and hydrogen per tonne of urea. Urea is made by 2NH3 + CO2 → NH2CONH2 + H2O. Molar masses: NH3 17, urea 60. NH3 per tonne urea = 1000 kg × (2×17)/60 = 567 kg. Making 567 kg NH3 from N2 + 3H2 needs 567 × 6/34 = 100 kg H2. Urea is 28/60 = 46.7 % nitrogen by mass, which is why it is the most used fertiliser in India's Green Revolution agriculture, with consumption of over 30 million tonnes a year, much of it made by plants fed with natural gas.

Bhopal, 2 to 3 December 1984. Water entered a storage tank of methyl isocyanate (CH3N=C=O), a reactive intermediate used for the pesticide carbaryl. The exothermic reaction pushed the tank's temperature and pressure up, and tens of tonnes of toxic vapour escaped to the sleeping city. Thousands died within days and hundreds of thousands were injured. The chemistry lesson: reactive, volatile intermediates should be stored in small amounts, kept dry and cool, with working safety systems, or avoided by a different synthesis.

Chlorine in the tap. Municipal water in Indian cities is chlorinated to leave a residual of roughly 0.2 to 0.5 mg/L at the consumer's end, enough to kill bacteria through HOCl without making water unpleasant.

In the Contact process, why is SO3 absorbed in conc. H2SO4 instead of being added directly to water?

SO3 dissolves in conc. H2SO4 to give oleum, H2S2O7, which is then diluted with water to the required strength in a controlled way. SO3 reacts readily with water, and the catalyst is V2O5.
