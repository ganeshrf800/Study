Biodiversity and Conservation

Species-area relationship, hotspots and conservation

Larger areas hold more species, in a predictable curve. Because some regions hold huge numbers of unique species in a small, threatened space, conservation prioritises hotspots and uses both in situ and ex situ methods.

  • Species-area (Alexander von Humboldt): S = C Az. On a log-log plot it is a straight line of slope z, usually 0.1 to 0.2 within a region and 0.6 to 1.2 across whole continents.
  • Diversity is highest in the tropics (more stable climate, more solar energy, more time to evolve). Four causes of loss (“evil quartet”): habitat loss and fragmentation, over-exploitation, alien invasions, co-extinctions.
  • A biodiversity hotspot has very high endemism (over 1,500 endemic vascular plants) and has lost at least 70% of its original habitat. The world has 34; India has parts of 4.
  • In situ: conserve in the natural habitat (national parks, sanctuaries, biosphere reserves, sacred groves). Ex situ: outside it (zoos, botanical gardens, seed banks, cryopreservation, in vitro fertilisation).
  • India has about 2.4% of the world's land area but about 8.1% of its species.

In the real world

The Amazon (about 6 million km²) and the Western Ghats (about 1.6 lakh km²) differ in area by 6,000,000 / 160,000 = 37.5 times. With z = 0.2, area alone predicts only 37.50.2 = e0.2 ln 37.5 = e0.725 ≈ 2.1 times as many species. The Amazon's real excess is much greater because it sits on the equator (warm, wet, stable), another reason diversity peaks in the tropics. Habitat loss: if a forest shrinks to 10% of its area with z = 0.2, species left = 0.10.2 = 0.63, so about 37% of species are lost. People protect habitat in many ways: Project Tiger (1973) created tiger reserves, Chipko (1973, Garhwal Himalaya, Gaura Devi and village women) hugged trees to stop felling, and sacred groves protect pockets of forest for local custom.
