Kirchhoff's laws and cells in a network

Series and parallel rules are not enough once a circuit has several batteries or loops. Kirchhoff's two laws, which are just conservation of charge and conservation of energy, let you write equations for any network and solve for every current.

  • Junction rule (KCL): the sum of currents entering a junction equals the sum leaving, because charge does not pile up at a point.
  • Loop rule (KVL): around any closed loop the algebraic sum of potential changes is zero, because the electric force is conservative.
  • Sign convention: going across a resistor in the direction of current is a drop (−IR); going through a cell from − to + is a rise (+E).
  • Choose current directions freely. A negative answer just means the current really flows the other way.
  • A real cell has internal resistance r, so its terminal voltage is V = E − Ir while it supplies current.

In the real world

Suppose a 12 V and a 6 V battery are wrongly connected in parallel across a 4 Ω load, with 2 Ω and 3 Ω of internal resistance. With the circuit above (E₁ = 12 V, R₁ = 2 Ω, E₂ = 6 V, R₂ = 3 Ω, R₃ = 4 Ω): I₃ = (12/2 + 6/3) / (1 + 4/2 + 4/3) = 8 / 4.33 ≈ 1.85 A. Then I₁ = (12 − 1.85×4)/2 ≈ 2.31 A, but I₂ = (6 − 7.38)/3 ≈ −0.46 A. The negative sign means the 6 V battery is being forced backwards, charged by the stronger one. Check: 2.31 − 0.46 = 1.85 A, as the junction rule requires.

This is why mismatched cells and batteries should never be paralleled, for example in a solar-park battery bank or an inverter battery pair: the stronger one drives large currents through the weaker one and heats it. Matching emf keeps both I₁ and I₂ positive.
